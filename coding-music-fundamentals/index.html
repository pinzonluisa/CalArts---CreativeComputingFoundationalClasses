Coding Music Fundamentals extends previous graphics related programming experience into the audio domain. ChucK, a strongly timed computer music language, is the focal language of the course, with opportunities to connect to physical computing platforms such as Arduino or graphics frameworks such as p5.js. The course explores several facets of digital audio, including synthesis, analysis, and interactive performance tools for live performance or composition.

This is explicitly not a music theory class, and students are never expected to arrive with or build musical training. A little shared vocabulary, pitch, rhythm, dynamics, timbre, makes the ChucK material land better, so Week 1 includes a light, informal music conversation and beginner friendly self guided tools. It’s context, not a prerequisite.

ChucK’s Strengths

ChucK’s own strengths point toward directions that are hard to do as well in any other tool, worth keeping in mind over the course of the semester as a way to push weekly sketches, the Midterm Project, or the Final Project beyond the most direct version of an exercise:

  • Data sonification. ChucK can map an arbitrary dataset, weather, seismic activity, biometric data, anything with numbers, directly onto pitch, filter cutoff, grain density, or any other sound parameter, live. Ryoji Ikeda’s datamatics and test pattern are the reference points.
  • Granular and microsound work. Live control over SndBuf playback position, rate, and windowing gets into granular synthesis fast, the clicks-and-cuts aesthetic of Alva Noto/Carsten Nicolai, or Ikeda’s more abrasive side, with a level of sample-accurate precision that’s fiddly to get in most visual tools.
  • Phase pieces. ChucK’s strongly timed model (shreds and now) makes it unusually easy to run near-identical voices that drift in and out of phase with each other, the same idea behind Steve Reich’s Piano Phase and Clapping Music, at a level of timing precision a human performer only approximates by ear.
  • Live coding. ChucK was built for on-the-fly code editing while a program runs, part of a whole performance tradition (TOPLAP, the live-coding/algorave scene) distinct from patcher or DAW based performance.
  • Networked, multi-performer pieces. ChucK grew out of Princeton’s PLOrk (laptop orchestra); several ChucK instances performing together is part of its own history.

Course Content

The course follows a series of eight pre-recorded video sessions on real time audio programming in ChucK. Starting in Week 2, each class period has two parts: first half, students share the assignment from the video they watched the previous week and the class listens to the work together; second half, the instructor previews the next video in plain language so students can ask questions before watching it and completing that week’s assignment on their own. Live class sessions, including any examples demonstrated in person, are recorded and posted in Canvas as additional material alongside the pre-recorded video series.

Midterm Project. This is where the material from the first six weeks, up through synthesis and physical modeling, comes together. See the Midterm Project page for the full assignment.

Final Project. The final project is done in groups, intentionally. Collaboration and co-creation are a core part of any art practice, and one of the things that keeps human made work different from anything a machine can produce on its own. Working in a group also builds skills every professional needs: communicating clearly, solving problems together, asking for help, and reaching decisions as a team. See the Final Project page for the full assignment.

Portfolio

A GitHub repository of ChucK code, alongside a recording of each performance on a platform such as Vimeo, SoundCloud, or YouTube, builds a working portfolio of code and sound, not files on a hard drive. The final project adds a digital recording of the performance as part of that same portfolio.

Future Pathways

The audio programming and signal processing foundations built in this course carry directly into later specializations, including:

  • Real time audio-visual performance systems
  • Interactive installation and sound design
  • Physical computing, interactive instruments, and robotics
  • Generative and algorithmic composition

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, BFA students will be able to:

  • Explain and apply core concepts in audio programming, sound synthesis, and signal processing using ChucK
  • Design and build original ChucK pieces, whether that’s a generative composition, an interactive audio work, an interface connecting to other systems, or music built from an outside data source
  • Build and prototype sound based systems using creative coding, hardware integration, and real time audio-visual environments
  • Connect a ChucK program to at least one other piece of software, using a protocol such as OSC or MIDI

MFA students, in addition, begin building toward:

  • Building original tools or techniques that go beyond what’s covered in class
  • Taking a leading technical role in a collaborative, cross disciplinary final project

Course Structure

  • Class length: 1 hour 50 minutes, officially starting 10 minutes after the posted time. The 10 minutes before that are an open window to ease in: grab a coffee, plug in your laptop, catch up with classmates, and get your head into a creative space.
  • Sign in for attendance at the start of every class.
  • Starting Week 2: first half, students share and the class listens to the assignment from the previous week’s video together; second half, a plain language preview of the next video so students can ask questions before watching it.
  • Hands-on practice and simple exercises throughout, to build confidence alongside the preview/review structure.
  • A 5–10 minute break partway through class.

Class Recordings

Live class sessions are recorded, including any examples demonstrated in person, and posted in Canvas as additional material alongside the pre-recorded video series. Recordings are a supplement, not a replacement, for the weekly watch-then-present structure described above.

Remote Attendance

A Zoom link is available on request when a student genuinely can’t be in the room: illness, a conflicting project obligation, something like that, as long as it’s requested ahead of time. Please don’t reach out the day of class about this; same-day requests usually won’t get seen in time, and the absence will count as unexcused. This is meant for real need, not routine use, so if it turns into a habit, the privilege goes away and absences get recorded as usual going forward.

Materials

Students need a laptop capable of running audio. Mac or PC are both fine, ChucK and miniAudicle run on either; support is fastest on Mac since that’s the instructor’s main setup, but Windows is just as welcome, flag any audio driver issues early. No installation is needed before Week 1: ChucK and miniAudicle are installed and sound-tested together in class on day one.

  • ChucK (free, open source) and the miniAudicle IDE
  • Chrome Music Lab, Ableton Learning Music, and BeepBox: free, browser based tools for Week 1 self study on rhythm, pitch, and timbre
  • Arduino Uno (or equivalent) and the Arduino IDE, for the Week 10 interconnection demo
  • The p5.js web editor and Ableton Live (or Live Lite/trial), also for Week 10
  • Access to a performance space for the Week 13 final group performance
  • A GitHub account, plus accounts on a hosting platform such as Vimeo, SoundCloud, or YouTube, for the Week 14 Digital Expo portfolio

No textbook purchase is required. See the Resources page for class links.

Submission Model

Each week’s assignment is presented live in class, with no digital submission: watching that week’s pre-recorded video and completing its exercise is the deadline. The Midterm and Final projects are both submitted digitally (through GitHub and a hosting platform) in addition to an in person presentation; see their own pages for details.

Grading

Weekly assignments: 50% Midterm project: 20% Final project: 30%

The weekly assignments category splits evenly across however many weekly assignments actually run that semester, not a fixed per-assignment percentage. Weekly assignments aren’t submitted digitally. Watching that week’s pre-recorded video, completing the assignment that goes with it, and presenting it in class is the deadline. Assignments not presented in class don’t receive a grade.
